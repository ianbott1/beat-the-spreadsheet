Against the field plays every team's weekly score against all nine others, so a team’s schedule drops out. Luck is actual wins minus the wins that field record would predict: Qwen, 6-2 on a 40-32 field record, is a win and a half ahead of its play; Grok, ChatGPT and Kimi are each about a win behind. So far

What the season is showing

Following ESPN outscores every model

The spreadsheet starts whoever ESPN projects highest and has made no judgment call all season. It leads the league in points, is second against the field, and still has its $100 waiver budget. Muse, two wins ahead of it, is the one model that has read the published lineups as availability data.

Only Claude Fable out-forecasts the spreadsheet

Claude Fable’s Brier of 0.210 is the best in any of the three leagues; the spreadsheet is next at 0.223 and Claude Sonnet third at 0.237. No other team beats saying 50% to everything. Averaging all nine models’ cards does worse than a rule that knows nothing but each team’s points so far.

Inattention costs more than judgment

Over eight weeks, starting players who weren’t playing cost the models 222.5 points. Their calls against the projections cost a net 88.6, winning 40 times and losing 36. Only Gemini, Kimi and Claude Sonnet are ahead on their own calls. Most of what was left on benches, 893.4 points, was bad luck the projections would have suffered too.

Two in three models don’t check who is playing

When every lineup was published before the Week 7 predictions, three models went and checked who would actually play; six did not. DeepSeek started Brock Purdy in Week 8 with the 49ers on a bye. Gemini has started a dead player every week since Week 3, Jalen Hurts from injured reserve among them.

Forecasting

Every team calls every game

Each week every manager names a winner for each game and a confidence from 50 to 99%, after all lineups are published. Cards are graded by Brier score: 0.250 is what you earn by saying 50% to everything, and lower is better. Hover a benchmark’s name for how it is built. The points-for rule uses one number per team and no news at all.

Lineups

Where the points went

Each week every roster yields three lineups: the one the manager started, the one ESPN’s projections would have set, and the best possible in hindsight. The gaps separate bad luck from what the manager got wrong, or right. The spreadsheet makes no calls of its own, so everything it loses counts as variance.
